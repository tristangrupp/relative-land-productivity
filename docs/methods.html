Methods

How the map uses satellite greenness to compare fields, adjust for weather and suggest land uses.

Most states use MODIS MOD13Q1, a 16-day greenness index (NDVI) at 250 m. A MODIS pixel is larger than 78 percent of Brazil’s fields, so readings from many fields include neighboring land and have low confidence. Mato Grosso, Pará, Rondônia and Acre use Sentinel-2 at 30 m. See methods/METHODS.md for the full method and pipeline/ANALYZE.md for implementation details.

We downloaded or streamed the source rasters and calculated the field values on one machine without Google Earth Engine. We used exactextract to find the share of each pixel inside each field, then applied those weights to every composite with a sparse matrix product.

2. The agricultural year

The agricultural year runs from 1 September to 31 August. The 2020 and 2024 years began on 1 September 2019 and 2023, respectively. This captures September soybean planting in Mato Grosso, Mato Grosso do Sul, Paraná, Santa Catarina and São Paulo, and August harvests of second-season maize. It also matches the MapBiomas convention, which labels temporary crops by the first growing cycle, typically peaking in January or February.

We use the 2024 field outlines for both years to compare the same land. The most common MapBiomas class within each outline gives the 2020 crop. We also score the original 2020 outlines for 2020 and mark fields abandoned if MapBiomas shows neither crop nor pasture there in 2024.

3. The growing season window

We use CONAB planting and harvest dates for each crop and state, from data/calendars/class_window_lookup.csv. The growing season can cross into the next calendar year. Temporary crop classes 19 and 41 share a season and peer group. Sugarcane, coffee, citrus, oil palm, other perennials and pasture use the full year. Forest plantations and unspecified agriculture also use the full year because the calendar lists neither.

4. Greenness per field and the seasonal integral

We exclude MODIS pixels with reliability flags of 2 or 3 (snow, ice or cloud) or quality flags of 2 or 3. For each composite, we average valid pixels, weighted by how much of each pixel is inside the field. If less than half the field has valid pixels, we use linear interpolation between dates to fill the gap. We save the count of observed composites as n_obs. Planetary Computer lacks the Terra composite for day 225 of 2024, so we interpolate it for every field.

For annual crops, the baseline is the lowest NDVI of the field’s 23 composites. It approximates bare soil. For perennial crops, the baseline is the 5th percentile across fields of the same class, state and year.

The seasonal integral adds up greenness above the baseline during the growing season. The formula is iNDVI = sum over the window of max(NDVI - baseline, 0). We weight each 16-day composite by 16 / 30.4375 months to give NDVI-months. The integral accounts for how long the canopy stays green, which helps distinguish dense fields where peak NDVI saturates. We also record peak NDVI, its timing and the full-year integral. Section 10 of METHODS.md lists the research behind the measure.

In Mato Grosso in 2024, soybean (116,914 fields) peaked a median of 4.8 months after 1 September, in late January. Its median peak NDVI was 0.884 and its seasonal integral was 2.45 NDVI-months. Pasture (948,056 fields) had a full-year integral of 2.88 above a baseline of 0.402.

5. Peer-relative productivity and the weather correction

Two steps limit the effect of weather on greenness scores.

Peer region. We group fields by peer class and year in the H3 cell that contains the field’s center (resolution 5, about 253 km² or 17 km across). Each group needs at least 30 agricultural fields with readings for at least half the growing season composites. Smaller groups use larger cells (resolution 4, 1,770 km², then resolution 3). Cells can cross state borders. Municipalities vary too much in size, from under 100 km² to 159,000 km².

Layer 1, peer-relative metric. R = (iNDVI - median of the group) / (1.4826 x MAD of the group), in robust peer standard deviations.

Layer 2, regression on weather anomalies. For each crop class and state, we regress the two years’ peer-group medians on standardized rainfall and temperature anomalies with quadratic terms. The anomalies use the mean and standard deviation of nine complete agricultural years, 2016 to 2024. The weather data start in January 2015, partway through the 2015 agricultural year. We fit a model only when at least 30 groups span both years. We subtract the fitted weather effect from each field after clipping its anomalies to the range in the fit and capping the effect at half the intercept. We fitted 134 of 176 crop and state pairs. The fits explained little (median R squared 0.15, quartiles 0.07 and 0.24, maximum 0.57), so the comparison with nearby fields does most of the work. See data/summaries/weather_fit.csv for the coefficients, R squared values and anomaly ranges.

We repeat the first step using the adjusted metric to get r_adj in peer standard deviations. The map shows prod = adjusted metric / peer median of the adjusted metric. A score of 1.0 is the peer median. Scores are capped at 0 and 5.

During the 2023 to 2024 El Niño drought, Mato Grosso’s growing seasons had median rainfall and temperature anomalies of -1.6 and +1.6 standard deviations. Comparing nearby fields reduces the effect of shared weather. Irrigation, planting dates, crop varieties, management and crop switches within a MapBiomas class can still affect scores.

6. Change classes

Change classes use D = r_adj 2024 - r_adj 2020 in peer standard deviations (SD), measured on the 2024 outline. The field card shows the change on the ratio scale, change = prod 2024 - prod 2020. Summary cell cards show the mean ratio-scale change and the difference between the shares of fields that declined and gained. The mean is within 0.08 of zero in 82 percent of cells. The difference in shares shows where declines outnumber gains.

7. Suggested decisions

Each field card shows one suggested action and other options. The method first compares crops and pasture in each 6 km H3 cell (resolution 6) with the same uses across Brazil. It then compares each field with nearby fields of the same crop. RESOLVE 2017 ecoregions provide additional context.

Area call

For each class in a cell, we compare its median weather-adjusted greenness with the national median, in standard deviations (SD) of cell medians at the same resolution. We weight classes by field count and combine them into crops and pasture. A class needs 30 fields. The cell card shows mosaic of uses, which is excluded from the calculation. “Below” means more than 0.75 SD below the national median. “Declining” means the gap grew by more than 0.4 SD from 2020 to 2024. At 6 km, 26 percent of pasture and 21 percent of crop cells are below, and 23 and 25 percent are declining. At 15 km, the shares are 28 and 13 percent below and 22 and 18 percent declining. Where no class meets the minimum, a 6 km cell uses the result of its 15 km parent. Of 144,909 cells, 100,122 use their own result (23,260,773 fields) and 44,787 use their parent’s (1,293,709 fields).

Too small to judge. The map flags fields under 6.25 ha for MODIS (one pixel) or 0.45 ha for Sentinel-2 (five pixels). Too few clear images also make a field low confidence. Since 14 November 2026, the map labels those fields “Low confidence: too small to judge yet.” The computed recommendation is still in the field card. In this tally, the rule applied to 19,464,886 fields (79.3 percent), including 223,502 in Mato Grosso.

Native vegetation and regrowth. We use MapBiomas Collection 10 to measure the share of native vegetation within 250 m and 1 km of the field’s bounding box. We calculate each share again for patches of at least 50 ha and record the largest patch within 1 km. The 50 ha cutoff excludes 20 percent of native pixels in the bands, including narrow strips along streams and roads. Regrowth is the share of the field mapped as crop, pasture or mosaic in 2020 and native vegetation in 2024. A field below its neighbors and stable or declining is a restore candidate if patches of at least 50 ha cover at least 30 percent of its 1 km band, the top quarter of these fields. The method suggests assisted natural regeneration if regrowth covers at least 10 percent of a declining field or a field marked for restoration, about the top 1 percent of fields. Elsewhere, higher yields can spare land.

Amazon biome. In the IBGE Amazon biome, the map suggests converting already cleared pasture to crops in a “Good land underused” cell if the field is at least as green as nearby pasture. For weaker pasture, it suggests improving pasture.

Pasture-only areas. The map suggests restoring declining fields in an “Area declining” cell where crops make up less than 5 percent of fields and no crop class reaches 30 fields. This rule identified 33,497 fields in seven Amazon frontier ecoregions, including 2,173 with high confidence. The previous rule identified none.

Satellite greenness suggests where to investigate. Visit the field and consider prices, soils, tenure, inputs and the farmer’s plans before deciding. The choice between restoration and higher yields relies on two years of greenness and two MapBiomas maps. Section 9 lists the limits.

Brazilian Forest Code in the restore recommendations (in preparation)

The Forest Code would affect the suggested treatment and the order of options in the field card. The map would show these changes through its existing classes and colors.

Article 12 of Brazil’s Forest Code (Law 12.651 of 2012) requires rural properties to keep a legal reserve of native vegetation. In the Legal Amazon, the reserve is 80 percent in forest, 35 percent in Cerrado and 20 percent in general fields. Elsewhere it is 20 percent. Owners with a shortfall must restore, regenerate or compensate for it.

The Rural Environmental Registry (Cadastro Ambiental Rural, CAR) catalog on source.coop includes 8,453,552 property outlines as of 16 July 2026. We match each 2024 field to the active or pending property with the largest overlap, provided it covers at least 20 percent of the field. We use the 2024 MapBiomas Collection 10 map on a 90 m grid to estimate native vegetation cover. IBGE boundaries set the estimate at 80 percent in the Amazon biome inside the Legal Amazon, 35 percent in the Cerrado biome inside it and 20 percent elsewhere.

On a field below its peers and next to native vegetation, restoration could help the owner meet a legal reserve requirement and qualify for funding. The estimate would favor restoration on properties with a shortfall and put higher yields first on properties that exceed the requirement.

This is a screening estimate. Landholders report CAR boundaries, which can overlap and mostly await validation. The law uses vegetation type to set the reserve, while the estimate uses biome. Properties up to 4 fiscal modules keep the vegetation they had on 22 July 2008 as their reserve and owe no restoration for earlier shortfalls. Pre-July 2008 clearings can be consolidated, owners can compensate for reserves on another property, and state zoning can lower the Amazon requirement to 50 percent. The estimate omits riverside protection areas because it lacks a national stream map at field scale. The map publishes only the derived indicators and omits property codes.

From 2020 to 2024, changes in r_adj had Spearman correlations with yield changes of 0.03 for maize, -0.02 for soybean and 0.08 for sugarcane. A test in two states found 0.38 for maize and 0.28 for soybean.

Raw greenness had a moderate correlation with yields for soybean in 2020 (0.46) and sugarcane in 2024 (0.48). The correlations for maize were close to zero. Every correlation for prod, r_adj and change was below 0.24. These scores rank fields against nearby peers. MODIS pixels mix field and neighboring greenness, and peer scores remove regional differences that help explain yields. Studies with finer pixels report 0.5 to 0.8 for greenness levels. Sentinel-2 reduces the mixing of neighboring land.

9. Limits of this release

  • Two years only. Restoration and yield suggestions rely on satellite greenness and MapBiomas data from 2020 and 2024. A bad season and a lasting decline can look the same.
  • Regrowth. MapBiomas regrowth means land mapped as crop, pasture or mosaic in 2020 and native vegetation in 2024. On 14 November 2026, the connection was too slow to download the 2023 map. Some apparent changes may reflect classification errors. This method misses older secondary vegetation.
  • Native vegetation band. We measure nearby vegetation from the field’s bounding box. We also cap a patch’s size at the edges of a processing strip about 61 km from north to south. A stream corridor connected to a large patch counts as part of it.
  • Sentinel-2 in Mato Grosso. We scale the 30 m Sentinel-2 change thresholds by 1.22, the ratio of year-to-year spread between sensors on fields over 25 ha with high confidence. On those fields, Sentinel-2 detects change on 22.5 percent and MODIS on 22.8 percent. Across all comparable fields, Sentinel-2 detects change on 23.2 percent and MODIS on 17.0 percent. Most of the difference comes from small fields that MODIS blends with neighboring land.
  • Pixel size. A MODIS pixel (250 m or 6.25 ha) is larger than 78 percent of fields. Those fields share readings with their neighbors. MODIS marks a field low confidence if it is under 6.25 ha or fewer than 75 percent of its growing season composites were observed in either year. In the original MODIS release, 20,344,905 of 24,554,482 fields (83 percent) had low confidence, including 684,199 of 1,151,506 in Mato Grosso.
  • Missing composite. Planetary Computer lacks the Terra composite for day 225 of 2024. We interpolate it for each field.
  • Wet-season gaps. In the MODIS data for Pará, wet-season composites had valid readings for 31 to 45 percent of fields. Fields with observations for fewer than half their growing season composites received no score (256,202 of 1,238,196 fields).
  • Pasture vigor is not implemented. MapBiomas Collection 10 pasture vigor data was unavailable as a public GeoTIFF, so pasture scores use NDVI alone.
  • Weak weather fit. The regression explains a median of 15 percent of variation in group medians. Comparing fields with local peers does most of the weather adjustment.
  • Validation. Municipal yield correlations are weak (section 8).
  • Mixed classes. Temporary crop classes 19 and 41 cover several crops. A switch from maize to beans within class 41 can look like a productivity change. The sugarcane harvest date affects the seasonal integral.
  • Outlines. A 2024 outline can average readings from two 2020 fields. The map simplifies field shapes to 0.0001 degree (one pixel) and shows the original field areas.
  • Low zooms. The tiles merge the smallest fields into their neighbors at zooms 9 to 11 to reduce file size. Every field appears from zoom 12.
